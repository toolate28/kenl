# Safe Spiral Redstone Museum: Educator Facilitation Guide
## How to Teach With Circuits (For Teachers, Parents, Facilitators)

---

## Core Principle: Let Discovery Do The Teaching

The most important thing to understand: **You are not explaining concepts. You are creating the conditions for kids to discover them.**

This is radically different from traditional teaching. You're not saying "Here's how probability works" and then showing Exhibit 2. You're saying "Watch these hoppers. What do you notice?" and then *they* discover that randomness has patterns.

This guide teaches you how to ask questions that enable discovery, rather than how to give explanations.

---

## The Universal Facilitation Pattern

Every exhibit follows this arc:

**1. Introduce Mystery** (No context, no rules)
- "What do you see?"
- Let them explore, ask questions
- Don't explain yet

**2. Guide Exploration** (Gently constrain their attention)
- "Try flipping each switch once"
- "Count how many items it takes"
- "How confident are you?"

**3. Let Them Discover** (They find the pattern)
- Watch. Don't intervene.
- They'll figure it out
- Mark the moment they gasp or say "Oh!"

**4. Name What They Discovered** (Now you explain)
- "What you just did is the scientific method"
- "That convergence to 36 is probability"
- "That's the Kelly Criterion you discovered"

**5. Connect to Bigger Picture** (Transfer understanding)
- "Neural networks work the same way"
- "Markets operate on this principle"
- "This is why AI sometimes fails"

- [ ] 1 facilitator per 3-5 kids (small groups = better discovery)

## Exhibit 1: Light Bulb - Facilitation Guide

**Time:** 30 minutes
**Age:** 8+
**Key Phrase:** "Which switch controls the light?"

### The Approach: Socratic Mystery

1. **Introduce (2 min)**
   - Point to the three switches
   - "These are labeled A, B, C"
   - Point to the lamp (distant, hard to see connection)
   - "One of these switches controls this light"
   - "Your job: figure out which one"
   - Don't mention the constraint yet

2. **Let Them Explore (10 min)**
   - Step back
   - Watch what they do
   - If they flip a switch: "What happened?"
   - If they flip another: "Is that the one controlling it?"
   - Guide their observation: "How did you figure that out?"

3. **The Discovery** (5 min)
   - They'll realize: "I can flip each one and see which works"
   - Let them test all three
   - The moment they say "It's Switch A!" is the discovery

4. **Name It** (5 min)
   - "Do you know what you just did?"
   - "You made a hypothesis (you thought it might be A)"
   - "You designed a test (flip it and watch)"
   - "You observed the result (light turned on)"
   - "You concluded (A controls it)"
   - "That's the scientific method"

5. **Transfer** (3 min)
   - "When scientists discover something new, they use this method"
   - "When AI researchers test a new theory, they use this method"
   - "You just became a scientist"

### Common Conversations

**Child:** "Why can I only test one switch at a time?"
**You:** "Good question! That constraint is what makes it interesting. What would happen if you could flip all three simultaneously?"
**Child:** "I'd see all three at once?"
**You:** "Exactly. Would that be harder or easier?"
**Child:** "Easier..."
**You:** "So the constraint actually forces you to be clever. That's the lesson."

**Child:** "I'm just guessing though, not being scientific."
**You:** "Are you? Let me ask: Did you make a prediction before you flipped the switch?"
**Child:** "Yeah..."
**You:** "And did you observe what actually happened?"
**Child:** "Yeah..."
**You:** "That's not guessing. That's testing. That's exactly what science is."

### Extension: Add Complexity

**For younger kids (8-10):**
- Simplify to 2 switches
- Make the lamp brighter or closer
- Celebrate discovery heavily

**For older kids (12-14):**
- Add a second challenge: "Now figure out which switch controls a *different* mechanism"
- Or: "Two switches control this light together—figure out which two"
- Challenge their logic: "How would you prove your answer to someone who doesn't believe you?"

## Exhibit 2: Double Sixes - Facilitation Guide

**Time:** 45 minutes (one run) or 2+ hours (multiple trials)
**Age:** 10+
**Key Phrase:** "How many items until both zones have items?"

### The Approach: Empirical Discovery

1. **Introduce** (3 min)
   - "This system drops items randomly into two zones"
   - "Count how many items it takes until BOTH zones have at least one"
   - "Can you guess before we start? Will it take 6? 20? 50?"
   - Write down their guess

2. **Run the Trial** (5-10 min)
   - Watch together
   - Keep a tally: "1 item, 2 items, 3 items..."
   - When both zones light up: "Stop! Count how many."
   - "You said [guess]. It actually took [count]. What do you think?"

3. **Repeat & Analyze** (20 min)
   - Run 10-20 trials
   - Write down each result
   - Create a simple tally: 12, 18, 9, 15, 22, 11, ...

4. **The Discovery** (5 min)
   - "Look at all these numbers"
   - "Are they different every time?"
   - "Yes, but notice: they're not *completely* random"
   - "Most are between 8 and 20"
   - "Calculate the average: (12+18+9+15+22+11+14+19) / 8 = ?"
   - "What's 6 times 6?" (if using dice context)
   - **The moment they realize:** "It's 36!"

5. **Name & Transfer** (5 min)
   - "That's probability. Randomness has patterns."
   - "We couldn't predict individual results, but we could predict the *average*"
   - "That's what the word 'probability' means: the pattern that emerges from randomness"
   - "AI systems learn to recognize patterns in randomness. That's machine learning."

### Key Insight to Convey

> "Randomness seems chaotic, but when you observe it long enough, patterns emerge. That's the entire foundation of statistics."

### Common Conversations

**Child:** "Why didn't it always take exactly 36?"
**You:** "Great question! The average was around 36, but each individual trial was different. Can you explain why that is?"
**Child:** "Because it's random?"
**You:** "Exactly! Each item drop is unpredictable. But the unpredictability itself follows a pattern. That's the paradox: randomness has order."

**Child:** "This is boring, the same thing keeps happening."
**You:** "The same thing IS happening—randomness IS predictable in aggregate. But here's the cool part: if I told you to design a system that does this reliably, could you do it? That's what engineers do."

### Extension: Advanced

**For interested kids:**
- "Run 50 trials. Does your average get closer to 36 with more data?"
- "Design an experiment: would it take fewer items if we had three zones? Why?"
- "Can you predict how long it takes for three zones to all have items?"

## Exhibit 3: Calibration Booth - Facilitation Guide

**Time:** 60 minutes total (30 min gameplay, 30 min analysis)
**Age:** 12+
**Key Phrase:** "How confident are you?"

### The Approach: Self-Awareness Through Data

1. **Introduce** (3 min)
   - "The booth will randomly pick one of four paths"
   - "You predict which one"
   - "You rate your confidence (1-15)"
   - "If you're right, you earn points = your confidence"
   - "If wrong, you lose points"
   - "20 trials. Can you beat 200 points?"

2. **Gameplay** (30 min)
   - Run all 20 trials
   - Record each: Confidence level, Prediction, Result, Points
   - Let them discover patterns (they can't predict the randomizer)
   - Watch them calibrate: Early trials they're confident and wrong; later they become more cautious

3. **The Analysis** (15 min)
   - Calculate: Average confidence claimed (across all 20 trials)
   - Calculate: Actual success rate (how often they were right)
   - Compare the numbers
   - **Most kids:** Claimed confidence 75%, actual success 50%
   - **The revelation:** "You were MUCH less accurate than you thought you were"

4. **The Conversation** (10 min)
   - "When you said 'confidence 14,' what did you think that meant?"
   - "But you were only right 50% of the time. So what does 'confidence 14' actually mean?"
   - "It means: you thought you were certain about something you had no way of knowing."
   - **The insight:** "Most people are overconfident. You just measured it."

5. **Transfer** (2 min)
   - "This is why experts say 'I'm 70% confident.' They measure their own uncertainty."
   - "AI systems should do the same. A model saying '99% confident' in a wrong prediction is dangerous."
   - "Knowing what you don't know is more important than being smart."

### Key Insight to Convey

> "Confidence is not the same as accuracy. Overconfidence destroys. Calibration saves."

### The Emotional Moment

This exhibit creates genuine emotional response. Kids see themselves overconfident and *feel* it. This is more powerful than any lecture about epistemic humility.

Don't diminish the emotion. Say: "Yeah, that feels bad. That's the point. Most people go through life overconfident and never measure it. You just measured it. That makes you ahead of most people."

### Common Conversations

**Child:** "This is impossible! How can I be right 50% if I'm guessing?"
**You:** "Good catch! You should be right 25% (random chance among 4 options). But you got 50%. Does that mean you're actually somewhat good at this?"
**Child:** "Maybe... but I said I was 80% confident!"
**You:** "Right. So your accuracy is about 50%, but your confidence was 80%. There's a gap. What does that gap mean?"
**Child:** "I thought I was better than I actually am?"
**You:** "Exactly. Most people do their whole lives and never realize it. You just realized it."

**Child:** "Can I run it again and try to be perfectly calibrated?"
**You:** "YES! Try the next 20 trials. See if you can get your confidence to match your actual success rate. That's the skill of calibration."

### Extension: Calibration Research

**For interested kids:**
- Run 100 trials, track calibration curve
- "Does your confidence improve (become more accurate) as you play more?"
- "Why might that be?"
- "How would you measure if you're getting better at calibration?"

## Exhibit 4: The Reroller - Facilitation Guide

**Time:** 60 minutes (50 rounds) + 10 minutes analysis
**Age:** 14+
**Key Phrase:** "When should you take the risk?"

### The Approach: Emergent Strategy Discovery

1. **Introduce** (5 min)
   - "Your goal: maximize score over 50 rounds"
   - "Each round: random value 0-20"
   - "Lock in: accept the value, add to score"
   - "Roll again: try for higher, but risk exceeding 21 (bust = lose the round)"
   - "Strategy: When should you risk? When should you play safe?"

2. **Gameplay** (40 min)
   - 50 rounds of decisions
   - Watch how their strategy evolves
   - Early rounds: Often too aggressive or too cautious
   - Middle rounds: Experimenting, finding patterns
   - Late rounds: Settled on a consistent threshold

3. **The Discovery Arc** (watch for this):
   - Rounds 1-10: "I'll try for maximum points" (high busts)
   - Rounds 11-20: "Maybe I should be careful" (missing upside)
   - Rounds 21-30: "There's a sweet spot..." (optimization beginning)
   - Rounds 31-50: "I know when to stop" (near-optimal strategy)

4. **Analysis** (10 min)
   - "What was your total score?"
   - "What was your average round value when you locked in?"
   - "Did you notice your strategy changed over time?"
   - "In early rounds you ____, but by round 40 you ____. Why?"
   - **Expected answer:** "I realized I should stop around 15-18 to maximize points"

5. **The Revelation** (5 min)
   - Introduce the Kelly Criterion formula (don't make them derive it, just show it exists)
   - "Mathematicians proved the optimal threshold is 16.8"
   - "You discovered through 50 rounds of playing that it should be ~15-18"
   - "You rediscovered mathematics just by optimizing"

6. **Transfer** (3 min)
   - "This is how real optimization works"
   - "You don't memorize the formula. You experiment, observe patterns, then discover the math describes what you found"
   - "AI researchers do this constantly"

### Key Insight to Convey

> "Optimal strategy isn't intuitive. It's discovered through iteration. Math predicts what experience discovers."

### Common Conversations

**Child:** "Why is there a 'right' strategy? Can't I just play how I want?"
**You:** "Absolutely! But if we compare your total score to someone who found the sweet spot, there's a difference. That difference is what optimization is: finding the choice that maximizes what you care about (your score)."

**Child:** "I busted every time I rolled again after 15."
**You:** "What does that tell you?"
**Child:** "That rolling again after 15 is a bad idea?"
**You:** "Exactly! Your experience taught you something. That's optimization. Keep playing and see if you can find the threshold where rolling again is actually worth it."

**Child:** "This is just luck."
**You:** "Is it? Look at your decisions. Are they random, or do they follow a pattern? Do you make the same choice in the same situation?"
**Child:** "The same choice..."
**You:** "That's not luck. That's strategy. And your strategy changed as you got better. That's learning."

### Extension: Advanced Strategic Analysis

**For interested kids:**
- Play with different bust thresholds (instead of 21, try 30)
- Does the optimal threshold scale proportionally?
- Design an experiment to find the *exact* optimal threshold
- Compare actual results to Kelly Criterion predictions

## Exhibit 5: Bid-Ask Spread - Facilitation Guide

**Time:** 60 minutes gameplay + 15 minutes analysis
**Age:** 16+
**Key Phrase:** "Why do spreads exist?"

### The Approach: Systems Thinking

1. **Introduce** (5 min)
   - "You're a market maker"
   - "Buyers and sellers can't see each other"
   - "Only you can see both sides"
   - "Your job: buy from buyers, sell to sellers, capture the spread"
   - "The spread is your profit. But holding the wrong inventory destroys you"

2. **Gameplay** (40 min)
   - 20 trades of market making
   - Watch them discover patterns:
     - High buyer pressure → buyers are desperate → raise your bid (buy at higher price)
     - High seller pressure → sellers are desperate → lower your ask (sell at lower price)
     - If you read the signals right: easy profit
     - If you hold inventory during price movement: losses

3. **The Discovery** (10 min)
   - "What did you earn? Were some rounds profitable and others not?"
   - "What made the difference between profitable and unprofitable rounds?"
   - "What would happen if you could see both the buyer and seller side simultaneously?" (hint at transparency)
   - Watch as they realize: "The spread would disappear"

4. **The Revelation** (15 min - this is the big moment)
   - "Show them both sides' prices publicly"
   - Literally collapse the spread to zero
   - "See? When information is shared, there's no profit opportunity for a middleman"
   - "The market maker's job isn't to be smarter. It's to *resolve information asymmetry*"
   - "The spread is payment for that information resolution"
   - "This is why markets need market makers, and why market makers need to be compensated"

5. **Connect to Information Architecture** (5 min)
   - "Remember: a system can only think about what it can attend to"
   - "Buyers can only attend to: their own desire to buy"
   - "Sellers can only attend to: their own desire to sell"
   - "Market maker attends to: both simultaneously"
   - "That asymmetric attention creates profit"
   - "When attention becomes symmetric, profit disappears"

### Key Insight to Convey

> "Markets exist to resolve information asymmetry. The spread is how we compensate those who do the resolution."

### The Moral Dimension

This exhibit addresses a common misconception: "Market makers are parasites extracting rents."

The truth: "Market makers are the infrastructure that makes trading possible. Without them, there is no market."

Convey this with intellectual honesty:
- "Market makers sometimes abuse information advantage (insider trading)"
- "That's wrong and illegal"
- "But the role itself—market making—is economically valuable"
- "This is the balance: compensation for providing liquidity, but with guardrails against manipulation"

### Common Conversations

**Child:** "Why would anyone accept a bad trade? Just refuse to buy or sell."
**You:** "Because they can't see the other side. If buyers could see seller pressure, they'd wait for better prices. But they can't see it. So the market maker—who can see it—has an advantage."

**Child:** "This seems like the market maker is exploiting them."
**You:** "That's one perspective. Here's another: Without the market maker, there is no trade at all. The buyer and seller have completely asymmetric information. The market maker bridges that gap, allowing trade to happen. The spread is how we compensate them for that service."

**Child:** "But when information becomes public, the spread disappears. Doesn't that mean the spread was unnecessary?"
**You:** "Great question! When information becomes public, maybe. But the market maker created that market. They took on risk. They resolved asymmetry. The spread compensated them. This is how systems find equilibrium: when a service is valuable, people pay for it. When it becomes commoditized, prices fall. That's healthy."

### Extension: Advanced Market Dynamics

**For researchers:**
- Add information cascades (false rumors that change prices)
- Add inventory constraints (what if you can only hold 5 items?)
- Add regulatory limits (what if bid-ask distance is restricted?)
- Challenge: Can you identify when a crash is coming and reduce inventory before it?

## Cross-Exhibit Facilitation: The Arc

The five exhibits build on each other cognitively. Here's how to facilitate the arc:

### Day 1: Foundations (Exhibits 1-2)
- **Light Bulb:** Hidden state teaches you that information asymmetry exists
- **Double Sixes:** Randomness teaches you that asymmetry creates uncertainty
- **Closing question:** "What happens when you can't see something?"
- **Answer:** "You have to guess, and your guess might be wrong"

### Day 2: Measuring Uncertainty (Exhibit 3)
- **Calibration Booth:** Uncertainty quantification builds on the previous day
- **Connection:** "Yesterday we saw information asymmetry creates uncertainty. Today we measure it."
- **Closing question:** "Should we make decisions based on our gut, or should we measure how good our gut actually is?"

### Day 3: Making Decisions Under Uncertainty (Exhibit 4)
- **The Reroller:** Strategy optimization
- **Connection:** "Once we measure uncertainty, we can optimize around it"
- **Closing question:** "Is there a best strategy? Or is it all luck?"
- **Answer:** "There's an optimal strategy. It's discovered through iteration."

### Day 4: Systems Coordination (Exhibit 5)
- **Bid-Ask Spread:** Information asymmetry in systems
- **Connection:** "Everything builds on everything else. Uncertainty → measurement → optimization → systems."
- **Closing question:** "How do complex systems coordinate when nobody has perfect information?"
- **Answer:** "Market mechanisms, prices, spreads—they're solutions to information asymmetry"

### The Capstone Conversation (After All Five Exhibits)

Bring the group together. Ask:

**"What did you learn across all five exhibits?"**

Listen for answers like:
- "Information matters"
- "You can't know everything"
- "Patterns emerge from randomness"
- "There are smart ways to make decisions"
- "Systems need mechanisms to work when people don't trust each other"

**Then say:**

"Exactly. This is the entire universe of intelligence. Not being smart. Being able to work with uncertainty. Finding patterns in noise. Making good decisions even when you don't know everything. And building systems that coordinate even when information is asymmetric.

This is what AI researchers grapple with. This is what traders deal with. This is what your brain does constantly.

And you just experienced all of it through Minecraft."

## Troubleshooting Guide

**Problem:** Kids are frustrated because they can't figure out the circuit
**Solution:** Ask better questions, don't explain. "What would happen if you tried [different action]?" Let them discover.

**Problem:** Kids are bored because they "get it" immediately
**Solution:** Add complexity. "Now do it backwards. Now do it with constraints. Now teach someone else how to do it."

**Problem:** Kids don't see the connection to AI
**Solution:** Be explicit. "When this circuit decides which path to take, it's similar to how an AI model decides between options. It's looking at signals and making choices."

**Problem:** Parent/teacher says "This is just a game, how does this teach anything?"
**Solution:** "Watch their faces when they discover the pattern. That's learning. They're not memorizing facts. They're discovering principles."

**Problem:** Technical issue—a circuit breaks during exhibit
**Solution:** Treat it as a teaching moment. "The circuit doesn't work. Why? Let's debug it together." (This is actually how engineers think.)

## Accessibility Considerations

### For Different Learning Styles

**Visual Learners:** Perfect for Redstone (visual circuits)
**Kinesthetic Learners:** Excellent (hands-on button pressing, decisions)
**Auditory Learners:** Pair with discussion/explanation
**Reading/Writing:** Provide guides, encourage note-taking

### For Different Ages

**Ages 8-10:** Focus on Exhibits 1-2 (observation, patterns)
**Ages 11-13:** Add Exhibit 3 (self-measurement)
**Ages 14-16:** Add Exhibits 4-5 (strategy, systems)
**Ages 17+:** All exhibits + research questions

### For Different Abilities

**High executive function:** Challenge them with research extensions
**Lower executive function:** Simplify exhibits, provide more scaffolding
**ADHD:** Break into shorter sessions, provide movement breaks
**Anxiety:** Emphasize "no right answer," focus on discovery not performance

## Final Teaching Principle

> "The circuit is the teacher. You're the guide."

Your job is not to explain Redstone or probability or calibration. Your job is to create conditions where kids *discover* these principles.

When a kid figures something out themselves, they own it. When you tell them, they forget it.

Let the circuits teach.

Your job is to ask: "What do you notice? Why do you think that happened? What would change if we modified it? Can you explain it to someone else?"

The circuits do the rest.
